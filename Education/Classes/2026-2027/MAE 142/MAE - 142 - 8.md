# Euler Angle Rates

- Recall that the Euler angles are defined by a sequential rotation order.
	- The rate of change in Euler angles is NOT the same as the body-axis rotational rates!

$$
\begin{array}{lll}
\begin{bmatrix}
p \\
q \\
r 
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \cos \phi & \sin \phi  \\
0 & -\sin \phi & \cos \phi
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{\phi} \\
0 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}+\dots \\  
\quad\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \cos \phi & \sin \phi \\
0 & -\sin \phi & \cos \phi
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\cos \theta & 0 & -\sin \theta \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
\sin \theta & 0 & \cos \theta
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
\dot{\theta} \\
0
\end{bmatrix}+\dots\\  
\quad\quad\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \cos \phi & \sin \phi \\
0 & -\sin \phi & \cos \phi 
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\cos \theta & 0 & -\sin \theta \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
\sin \theta & 0 & \cos \theta
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\cos \psi & \sin \psi & 0 \\
-\sin \psi & \cos \pi s & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
0 \\
\dot{\psi}
\end{bmatrix}
\end{array}
$$

# Euler Differential Equations

- The Euler angle differential equations can be expressed in matrix/vector format.
	- However, this matrix form does NOT represent an orthogonal transformation!

$$
\begin{array}{c} 
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}
p \\
q \\
r 
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -\sin \theta \\
0 & \cos \phi & \sin \phi \cos \theta \\
0 & -\sin \phi & \cos \phi \cos \theta
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{\phi} \\
\dot{\theta} \\
\dot{\psi}
\end{bmatrix}} \\
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{\phi} \\
\dot{\theta} \\
\dot{\psi}
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
1 & \sin \phi \tan \theta & \cos \phi \tan \theta \\
0 & \cos \phi & -\sin \phi \\
0 & \sin \phi /\cos \theta & \cos \phi /\cos \theta
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
p \\
q \\
r
\end{bmatrix}}
\end{array}
$$
# Angular Rates and Euler Angles

- Body-axis angular rates (p,q,r) are not the same as the Euler angle rates of change.
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{\phi} \\
\dot{\theta} \\
\dot{\psi}
\end{bmatrix}\neq \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
p \\
q \\
r
\end{bmatrix}
$$
- Example: p=r=0, theta=0, phi = 90 deg

$$
\begin{array}{rcc}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\phi}\\ \dot{\theta}\\ \dot{\psi}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}1 & \sin\phi\tan\theta & \cos\phi\tan\theta\\ 0 & \cos\phi & -\sin\phi\\ 0 & \sin\phi/\cos\theta & \cos\phi\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}p\\ q\\ r\end{bmatrix}
& \xrightarrow{\qquad} &
\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\phi}\\ \dot{\theta}\\ \dot{\psi}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}q\sin\phi\tan\theta\\ q\cos\phi\\ q\sin\phi/\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
\\[6pt]
& & \Big\downarrow \\[6pt]
\begin{array}{l}\text{Rotation about the y-body}\\ \text{axis changes vehicle}\\ \text{heading angle only.}\end{array}
& &
\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\phi}\\ \dot{\theta}\\ \dot{\psi}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0\\ 0\\ q\end{bmatrix}
\end{array}
$$

# Quaternion Differential Equations

- The quaternion differential equations do not require trigonometric functions and therefore require less computational effort than the Euler differential equations.
	- Quaternion equations also avoid singularities that can occur in the Euler equations when the pitch angle is close to ninety degrees.

$$
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{b}_{0} \\
\dot{b}_{x} \\
\dot{b}_{y} \\
\dot{b}_{z}
\end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}
-b_{x} & -b_{y} & -b_{z} \\
b_{0} & -b_{z} & b_{y} \\
b_{z} & b_{0} & -b_{x} \\
-b_{y} & b_{x} & b_{0} 
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
p \\
q \\
r
\end{bmatrix}}\quad
\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}
\dot{b}_{0} \\
\dot{b}_{x} \\
\dot{b}_{y} \\
\dot{b}_{z}
\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}
0 & -p & -q & -r \\
p & 0 & r & -q \\
q & -r & 0 & p \\
r & q & -p & 0
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
b_{0} \\
b_{x} \\
b_{y} \\
b_{z}
\end{bmatrix}}
$$

# Attitude Reference Systems

- Angular rate gyros are available to measure body-axis rotational rates (p,q,r).
	- Measurement noise prevents use of numerical integration techniques to find Euler angles using only rate gyros.

# Angular Rate Equations

- The Euler angle differential equations can also be used to estimate the three angles using measurements of the body-axis angular rates.
$$
\begin{array}{l}
\dot{\phi}=p+\tan \theta(q\sin \phi+r\cos \phi) \\
\dot{\theta}=q\cos \phi-r\sin \phi \\
\dot{\psi} =(q\sin \phi+r\cos \phi) /\cos \theta 
\end{array}
$$
- A second estimate of the Euler angles is obtained by numerical integration:
$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\boxed{\begin{array}{l}
\hat{\phi}=\phi+\Delta[p+\tan \theta(q\sin \phi+r\cos \phi)] \\
\hat{\theta}=\theta+\Delta[q\cos \phi-r\sin \phi] \\
\hat{\psi}=\psi+\Delta[(q\sin \phi+r\cos \phi) /\cos \theta]
\end{array}} 
  & \begin{array}{c}
  \text{From last time point: }  \\
  \phi,\theta,\psi,p,q,r \\
  \Delta=\text{time step since last point}
  \end{array}
\end{array}
$$

# Complimentary Filter

- The final Euler angle estimate is a blend of the two solutions.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\boxed{\begin{array}{l}
\phi = k\tilde{\phi}+(1-k)\hat{\phi} \\
\theta=k\tilde{\theta}+(1-k)\hat{\theta} \\
\psi=k\tilde{\psi}+(1-k)\underset{ k\in[0,1] }{ \hat{\psi} }
\end{array}} &  
\begin{array}{ll}
(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\psi}) & \text{Euler angles estimated from \\accelerometer and magnetometer} \\
(\hat{\phi},\hat{\theta},\hat{\psi})  & \text{Euler angles estimated from rate\\gyros.}
\end{array}
\end{array}

$$
- Choose k close to unity if accel/magnetometer solution is ore reliable.
	- Accelerometer signals tend to be very noisy
- Choose k close to zero if the rate gyro solution is more reliable.
	- Numerical integration of noisy gyro data leads to drift in Euler angle estimates
