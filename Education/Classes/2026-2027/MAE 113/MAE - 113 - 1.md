## Introduction to Propulsion
### Scope of Thermodynamics
- Study of Thermodynamics begins with our Laws--The Zeroth, First, Second, and the Third Law of Thermodynamics
	- The laws are facts of experience
	- No mathematical proof exists
	- Validity lies in the absence of contrary experience
- From these laws one can derive a network of equations that have application in physical sciences and engineering. 
### System & Surroundings
- System
	- Is the topic of attention
	- Could be a furnace, automobile engine, jet engine or rockets
- Surroundings
	- The rest of the universe that is not included in the system. 
	- Observations of the system are made from the surroundings.

### State Functions and Path Functions 
- State Functions
	- Properties that do not depend on the past history of the substance nor on the means by which it reaches a given state. Examples pressure ($p$), temperature ($T$).
- Path Functions
	- Quantities such as "heat" and "work" are not properties, they account for energy changes that occur in the surroundings (not the system)

#### State Functions
- The Laws of Thermodynamics are used to define the changes in the intensive state functions, pressure, $p$, and temperature, $T$,  and the extensive state functions internal energy, $U$, and entropy, $S$.
- The extensive state functions enthalpy, $H$, Gibbs free energy, $G$, and Helmholtz free energy, $A$ are defined for convenience.

### First Law of Thermodynamics 
- The 1st law of thermo, is an extension of the law of conservation of energy (energy cannot be created nor destroyed).
- Clarifies energy concepts
- **The internal energy of an isolated system is constant$$
	 \Delta U = Q+ W
	$$
- For small changes (calculus):
$$
\delta U = \delta Q + \delta W
$$

### Second Law of Thermodynamics
- **There exists a absolute scale for temperature $T$ and an extensive function entropy $S$, such that for an infinitesimal process in a closed system $$
T \delta S_{\text{system}}\geq \delta Q
$$where the equality holds for reversible processes and the inequality is valid for natural processes. Here $\delta Q$ is heat transferred to the system from the surroundings. 
- As far as the surroundings are care, the heat transfer is reversible. Hence $\delta S_{\text{surroundings}}=-\delta Q/T$ 
- Hence it follows that $$
\delta S_{\text{universe}}=\delta S_{\text{surroundings}}+\delta S_{\text{system}}\geq 0

$$
### Fundamental Equations of Chemical Thermodynamics

From the first and second law of thermodynamics
$$
\begin{gather}
\delta U   & = &   \delta Q &  + &  \delta W \\
T \delta S  &  \geq & \delta Q \\
\end{gather}
$$
For a reversible process
$$
\begin{gather}
\delta W_{\text{rev}}  &   =  & -p\delta V \\
\delta Q_{\text{rev}} & = & T\delta S \\
\end{gather}
$$
Hence
$$
\delta U = T\delta S - p \delta V
$$
The equation 
$$
\begin{align*}
\boxed{\delta U = T\delta S - p\delta V} \tag{4}
\end{align*}
$$
is valid for all processes because it relates state functions. Define for convenience, the extensive functions enthalpy, $H$, Gibbs Free Energy $G$, and Helmholtz Free Energy, $A$ as
$$
\begin{gather}
H  &  \equiv  &  U+pV \\
G  &  \equiv  &  H-TS  \\
A  &  \equiv  &  U - TS  \\
\end{gather}
\tag{5}
$$
it follows
$$
\begin{align*}
\boxed{\delta  H    =    T\delta S + V\delta p} \tag{6} \\
\boxed{\delta G   =   -S \delta T + V \delta p} \tag{7} \\
\boxed{\delta A    =   -S\delta T - p \delta V} \tag{8}
\end{align*}
$$

Equations (4), (6), (7), (8) are called the fundamental equations of chemical thermodynamics.

### Notation
- In the subsequent development, $V,U,S,H,G,A,$ and will be the volume, internal energy, entropy, enthalpy, Gibbs free energy and Helmholtz free energy per mol respectively.
- Q and W will represent heat addition to the system and work done on the system per mol.

### Heat Capacity

Introduce the following definitions for the heat capacity, $C_{p}$, and $C_{v}$
$$
C_{\text{p}} \equiv \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial T} \right)_{p}; \quad C_{\text{V}} \equiv \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} \right)_{V}
\tag{9}

$$
- For a constant volume process from Eq. (9) $\delta H = C_{\text{p}}\delta T$. If only work due to expansion or compression is considered, for constant V, $\delta W = 0$, as a consequence $\delta U = \delta Q$. Hence $\boxed{C_{\text{V}}\delta T=\delta Q}$ 
- For a constant pressure process form Eq. (9) $\delta H = C_{\text{p}}\delta T$. Also $\delta H = \delta U = p \delta V + V \delta p = \delta Q + \delta W + p \delta V + V \delta p$ . For a reversible process $\delta W_{\text{rev }}= -p\delta V$. Hence for a constant pressure reversible process $\delta H = \boxed{C_{\text{p}}\delta T = \delta Q}$.

### Criteria for Chemical Equilibrium 
Consider a closed system that is not in chemical equilibrium and in which chemical reactions are taking place. 
- From the statement of the second law of thermodynamics $$
\Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S _{\text{surr}} \geq 0
$$
- At equilibrium $\Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} = 0$. Hence the fundamental criteria for chemical equilibrium is that the total entropy of the universe which is the sum of the entropy of the system and entropy of the surroundings must be a maximum. 
- If the system is isolated,
	- It does not interact with the surroundings. Hence $\Delta S_{\text{surr}}=0$, $\Delta S_{\text{sys}}=0$
	- Hence for an isolated system at chemical equilibrium the entropy of the system, $S_{\text{sys}} = \text{maximum}$
