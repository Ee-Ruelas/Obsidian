$$
\begin{gather}
\text{const } \kappa + \text{steady state } + \dot{Q}_{gen} = 0  \\
\dot{Q}_{in} = \dot{Q}_{out}=\dot{Q} \\
\end{gather}
$$
![[MAE - 101C - 2-1791920520360.webp|394]]

$$
\begin{gather}
\dot{Q} = \frac{T_{1}-T_{2}}{\frac{L_{A}}{\kappa_{A}A}} = \frac{T_{2}-T_{3}}{\frac{L_{B}}{\kappa_{B}A}} \quad \text{Since } \dot{Q} = \text{const.} \\
\begin{rcases}
\dot{Q}\left( \frac{L_{A}}{\kappa_{A}A} \right) = T_{1}-T_{2} \\
\dot{Q}\left( \frac{L_{B}}{\kappa_{B}A} \right)=T_{2}-T_{3} \\
\end{rcases} \text{Add two equations}  \\
\dot{Q}\left[ \frac{L_{A}}{\kappa_{A}A}+\frac{L_{B}}{\kappa_{B}A} \right]=T_{1}-T_{3} \\
\dot{Q} = \frac{T_{1}-T_{3}}{\left( \frac{L_{A}}{\kappa_{A}A} \right)+\left( \frac{L_{B}}{\kappa_{B}A} \right)} = \frac{T_{1}-T_{3}}{R_{A}+R_{B}} \\
\boxed{\dot{Q}=\frac{\Delta T}{R_{A}+R_{B}}} \\

\end{gather}
$$

$$
\begin{gather}
\text{For multiplier composite} \\
\dot{Q} = \frac{\Delta T}{\sum R}
\end{gather}

## Stefan-Boltzmann's Law of Radiation - Ch.1.3.2

### Thermal Radiation

Radiation travels as electromagnetic waves (photons) and needs no medium.


$$
\begin{gather}
\text{Wavelength } \lambda [\mathrm{{m}}, \mathrm{{\mu m}}] \text{ and Frequency } \nu[\mathrm{{s}}^{-1}] \\ \\

\lambda \nu=c \\
c \approx 3\times 10^{8}\mathrm{{m/s}}\text{ (speed of light)} \\
\end{gather}
$$

Every surface above ${}0\mathrm{{K}}{}$ emits radiation, all the time. Radiation is the only mode that works in vacuum, which is how the Sun heats the Earth and how satellites reject heat. 

### Where is Thermal Radiation on the Spectrum?

![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792103441277.webp]]

**Thermal radiation** is emitted because of a body's **temperature**. It covers part of the UV, all of the visible and much of the infrared. Near room temperature almost all of it is infrared, which is why the IR band is often called "heat".

### Irradiance ${}G{}$ and Radiosity ${}J{}$

![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792103813241.webp|344x275]]
- Irradiance ${}G{}$
	- Radiant energy flux arriving at (incident on) the surface.
	- ${}[\mathrm{{J}}/\mathrm{{m^{2}\cdot s}}] = [\mathrm{{W}}/\mathrm{{m^{2}}}]{}$

- Radiosity ${}J{}$
	- All radiant energy flux leaving the surface:
	- ${}J = {}$emitted ${}+{}$ reflected ${}[\mathrm{{W}}/\mathrm{{m^{2}}}]{}$

Watch out: an IR thermometer measures radiosity, which includes reflected radiation. Shiny surfaces reflect a lot, so their readings can be badly wrong.

### The Black Body (Black Surface)

Black Body = B.B ${}\Rightarrow{}$ ${}J_{B.B}={}$emitted ${}+{}$ ~~reflected~~

1. Perfect absorber
	- Absorbs all radiation incident on it; reflects and transmits none.
2. Perfect emitter
	- Emits the maximum possible radiation at its temperature ${}T{}$. No real surface at the same ${}T{}$ emits more.

So for a black body, radiosity = emission only: ${}J=E_{b}{}$. It is an idealization, but many surface come close.

### For a Black Body: Stefan–Boltzmann Law

$$
\begin{gather}
J = E_{b} = \sigma T^{4} \\
\text{\tiny{(black body only)}}
\end{gather}

$$
${}J{}$ : Radiosity
${}E_{b}{}$: Black-body emissive power ${}[\mathrm{{W}}/\mathrm{{m^{2}}}]{}$
${}\sigma{}$: Stefan–Boltzmann constant ${}= 5.67\times 10^{-8} \mathrm{{W}}/(\mathrm{{m}}^{2}\cdot \mathrm{{K}}^{4}){}$
${}T{}$: absolute surface temperature ${}[\mathrm{{K}}]{}$


*Always use kelvin!*
Because of the fourth power, using ${}^{\circ}C{}$ gives wrong numbers!

### How Strongly Does Emission Depend on T?

![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792104944626.webp|335x326]]
$$
\begin{gather}
T \times 10  \\
\small{(300\to 3000\mathrm{{K}})} \\
\Rightarrow E_{b}\times 10,000
\end{gather}

$$
Radiation is often negligible near room temperature next to forced convection, but it dominates in furnaces, combustion, and space.

### Real Surfaces: A Sheet of Glass

![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792105088226.webp|432x340]]

$$
\begin{gather}
\text{Energy balance on the incident radiation:} \\
G = G_{R} + G_{A} + G_{T} \\
 \\
[G = G_{R} + G_{A} + G_{T}]/G \\
1 = \frac{G_{R}}{G} + \frac{G_{A}}{G} + \frac{G_{T}}{G} \\
1 = \rho + \alpha+ \tau
\end{gather}
$$
Only the absorbed part ${}G_{A}{}$ changes the glass's internal energy. Reflected and transmitted radiation pass it by. 

$$
\begin{gather}
\rho   & =  &  G_{R}/G &  \text{ Reflecitvity(reflectance)}\\
\alpha  &  =  &  G_{A}/G  & \text{ Absorptivity (absorptance)} \\
\tau  &  =  & G_{T}/G   & \text{ Transmissivity (transmittance)}
\end{gather}
$$
$$
\begin{gather}
\boxed{\rho + \alpha+\tau=1} \\
\end{gather}
$$
This is called total (overall) properties of the surface averaged over all wavelengths. Each lies between 0 and 1. 

### Spectral (Monochromatic) Properties

${}\rho_{\lambda} + \alpha_{\lambda} + \tau_{\lambda} = 1{}$ Holds at every single wavelength. The properties themselves can change a lot with ${}\lambda{}$. 

![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792105666025.webp]]

### Opaque, Black, and Real Surfaces

- Opaque
	- Nothing transmitted: ${}\tau=0{}$
		- ${}\rho + \alpha = 1\Rightarrow \alpha = 1- \rho{}$
- Black
	- ${}\tau = 0, \rho =0 \Rightarrow \alpha = 1{}$
		- Absorbs everything and emits the maximum, ${}E_{b}=\sigma T^{4}{}$
- Real
	- In general: ${}\alpha \neq 0, \rho \neq 0, \tau \neq 0{}$
		- A real surface at ${}T{}$ emits less than a black surface at the same ${}T{}$

"Opaque" depends on wavelength: a surface can be opaque in one band and transparent in another. 


### Opaque or Not?

1. Is a wooden table opaque to visible radiation?

Yes because you can't see through the other side. 

2. Window glass is transparent to visible light. Is it transparent to the ${}~10\mathrm{{\mu m}}{}$ infrared that your room emits? ![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792108384156.webp]]

No, based on the picture at ${}10 \mathrm{{\mu m}}{}$ the radiation is mostly absorbed and not transmitted like visible light. 

### Emissivity ${}\varepsilon{}$ of a Real Surface

A real surface at temperature ${}T{}$ emits less than a black body at the same ${}T{}$. We define emissivity or emittance: 

$$
\begin{matrix}
\varepsilon = (E/E_{b})_{T}  &   &  E = \varepsilon \sigma T^{4} \\
\text{ratio at the smae temperature} &  & \underset{[\mathrm{{W}}/\mathrm{{m^{2}}}]}{\text{emissive power of a real surface}}
\end{matrix}
$$
Range
- ${}0 \leq \varepsilon \leq 1{}$
- ${}\varepsilon = 1 {}$ for a black body
- Real surfaces have ${}\varepsilon<1{}$

What does it depend on?
${}\alpha, \rho, \tau, \varepsilon{}$ all depend on the surface (material, finish, oxidation), its temperature ${}T{}$, and the wavelength ${}\lambda{}$.

### The Gray Surface

![[MAE - 101C - 2-1792109274416.webp|365]]

- Gray Surface
	- Its emissivity is the same at every wavelength (and independent of temperature).
	- ${}\varepsilon=\alpha<1{}$
	- A good absorber is a good emitter

*Most engineering calculations assume gray surfaces, so one number ${}(\varepsilon){}$ describes both emission and absorption*
